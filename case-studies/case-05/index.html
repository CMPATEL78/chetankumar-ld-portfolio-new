01. The Design Challenge

Throughout my academic career, I have worked with learning at different levels of ownership. Sometimes the institutional curriculum was already defined and my role was to improve how the subject was experienced and learned. In other cases, I substantially modified the content and assessment. In some cases, I designed the complete course architecture from the ground up.

The central distinction: What must be taught and how it should be learned.

This approach has been applied across Mechanical Engineering, Computing, Programming, Creativity and Innovation, and cross-disciplinary capability development.

02. My Course Design Philosophy

Learner→Need→Learning Objective→Content→Experience→Practice→Assessment→Evidence

The level of ownership may vary, but the focus remains the same: convert subject content into a learning experience that enables the learner to understand, practise and apply.

04. Theory of Machines

Enhancing an Existing Technical Curriculum. The institutional syllabus provided the formal subject structure. My role was to substantially enhance the learning experience through content modification, visualisation, simulation, practical work and problem-based learning.

What I redesigned

  • Modified and enhanced selected topics
  • Designed the laboratory manual and conducted the laboratory component
  • Developed graphically rich presentations
  • Integrated simulations and videos
  • Introduced problem-based learning and assignments
  • Designed and implemented assessment activities
  • Conducted final evaluation
  • Adapted delivery for online learning during COVID-19

Learning architecture

Concept→Visualisation→Simulation→Problem→Practice→Assessment

The intention was to make difficult mechanical concepts more accessible through visual and practical representation.

Evidence chain: Curriculum → Content Enhancement → Visual Presentation → Simulation → Problem-Based Learning → Assignment → Assessment → Final Evaluation.

05. Kinematics of Machines

Turning Technical Concepts into Design Problems. The course followed an institutional syllabus, while the learning experience and application of the content were redesigned using graphical presentations, simulations, videos, problem-based learning, structured problem solving, assignments, practical work and online delivery where required.

Distinctive assessment: students were asked to design an innovative mechanism for outdoor fitness equipment using AutoCAD. The assessment therefore moved beyond reproduction of kinematics knowledge toward application.
Kinematics Concept→Mechanism→Real-world Design Problem→AutoCAD→Student-created Solution

This became an application-oriented assessment rather than a purely theoretical test.

06. Logic Building Techniques

Making a Challenging First-Year Subject Approachable. Logic Building Techniques was a first-year B.Tech subject delivered across eight divisions, with different subject partners. Some sessions involved groups of approximately 120 students and could last up to two hours. Learners came from different states and countries with varying levels of familiarity and confidence.

Making logic visible

  • Colourful PPT presentations
  • Graphical flowcharts and algorithms
  • Pen-tablet demonstrations
  • Classroom demonstrations
  • Handouts

Design objective

Make abstract logical structures visible and approachable while maintaining engagement across a large and diverse learner population.

07. Creative Assessment for Logic Building

One internal examination was redesigned as a poster-making activity. Students used colours to represent flowcharts, algorithms and pseudocode.

Understand→Represent→Create→Explain→Demonstrate Understanding

The assessment became an opportunity to demonstrate understanding while reducing the tension associated with a conventional examination environment.

08. Redesigning the Final Examination

The university normally used a standardized written-examination structure. I identified limitations of that format for Logic Building Techniques and obtained special permission to substantially change the examination structure.

Assessment redesign cycle: Identify limitation → Propose change → Obtain approval → Redesign → Implement.

The conventional two-section, 2/4/6-mark structure with optional selection was replaced with a format designed to place greater emphasis on understanding and logical thinking rather than memory.

09. From Teaching Practice to a Content Product

The classroom challenges and solutions developed while teaching Logic Building Techniques eventually led to a book. The book documents teaching methods, challenges and solutions developed through classroom experience and is intended as a resource for both teachers and students.

Teaching challenge→Experimentation→Refined method→Documented methodology→Reusable content

The book is therefore evidence not only of authorship, but of converting instructional practice into reusable learning content.

10. Programming with Python

From Learning Concepts to Demonstrable Capability. Before teaching the course, I developed the required Python capability through online learning, ChatGPT and Google Colab implementation. The course was then structured around implementation rather than passive understanding.

Concept→Example→Live Coding→Practice→Debugging→Problem Solving→Working Output→Application

Learning methods

Real-world programming problems, step-by-step coding demonstrations, live coding, flowcharts and pseudocode, debugging demonstrations, progressive exercises, mini-projects and everyday-context examples. Classroom learning used PPTs, demonstrations and handouts; laboratory learning involved students solving and submitting programming problems.

11. Authentic Computer-Based Assessment

The assessment was designed to measure actual programming/problem-solving capability and was 100% computer based. Students worked directly on laboratory workstations.

Representative tasks

  • Complete missing code
  • Find logical errors
  • Identify syntax errors
  • Solve task-based programming problems
  • Find program output

Controlled environment

  • No internet support
  • Workstation-based execution
  • Submission through Canvas LMS
  • Separate evaluation of laboratory work
Assessment question: Can the learner actually work with code, rather than simply describe programming concepts?

12. Assessment at Scale

A major operational challenge was conducting the assessment for approximately 480 students with limited resources.

Authenticity+Standardisation+Controlled Conditions+Digital Submission

This makes the Python course an example of learning design combined with assessment operations at scale.

13. Creative Thinking Habits

Complete Course Design from the Ground Up. This is the strongest example in this module of full course ownership. The purpose was to deliberately develop creativity and innovation capability rather than treating creativity as something that would emerge automatically from technical education.

A previous two-week creativity workshop provided a foundation for further exploration. The course was intentionally designed for cross-disciplinary relevance rather than being restricted to Mechanical Engineering.

14. A Creativity Toolkit

The course introduced learners to a broad toolkit of 26 creativity and thinking techniques, including:

Problem exploration & reframing

Generating W-H Questions, Bugs Finding Exercise, CIA Method, Search the Space, Transfer the Problem, Reverse the Technique, Search & Squeeze, Topling.

Idea generation

BFF, Worst Idea, SCAMPER, Connecting Unconnected, Analogy, Play, Brainstorming, Host an Idea, Practice of Making.

Thinking & analysis

Mind Mapping, Fish Bone Diagram, Thinking Fluency — Divergent / Convergent Thinking, Heuristic Method, KT Method, CORT Tools, Six Thinking Hats.

Design & innovation

Design Thinking and the reflection habit: Reflect.

The breadth of the toolkit allowed different techniques to be selected according to the problem rather than forcing every problem through a single creativity method.

15. Designing the Learning Environment

Before the semester began, students were required to obtain a dedicated creativity kit containing coloured papers, sketch pens, sticky notes, coloured cards, highlighters, Fevicol and drawing sheets.

Learning environment principle: prepare the learner to draw → arrange → connect → modify → create, rather than simply listen.

16. The Creative Thinking Classroom Cycle

01Technique explained
02Relevant example
03New problem introduced
04Group application
05Real-time observation / evaluation
Core principle: Do not merely explain creativity. Make learners practise it.

Learning and reflection followed the application stage, making practice an integral part of instruction.

17. Embedded Assessment

There was no separate internal examination. Students were evaluated during the activities themselves.

Learning→Practice→Application→Observation→Evaluation

Assessment became part of the learning experience rather than an event disconnected from it.

18. Summative Project Assessment

At the end of the semester, learners completed two mini-projects. They selected problems from a pool of problems, applied creativity techniques and submitted their work through Canvas LMS.

Creativity Techniques→Guided Practice→Group Problem Solving→Independent Application→Mini Project 1→Mini Project 2→Canvas Submission

This allowed learners to demonstrate whether they could apply creative-thinking methods to unfamiliar problems.

19. The Common Design Pattern

Although the five courses are very different, a common design philosophy appears across them:

CourseLearning progression
Theory of MachinesConcept → Visualisation → Simulation → Problem → Practice → Assessment
KinematicsConcept → Application → Mechanism → AutoCAD → Solution
Logic BuildingConcept → Visual Representation → Practice → Creative Assessment → Understanding
PythonConcept → Code → Debug → Problem Solve → Working Output
Creative ThinkingTechnique → Example → New Problem → Group Practice → Project
The subject changes. The learner changes. The tools change. But the underlying design principle remains: move the learner from receiving information to doing something with it.

23. Professional Capability Demonstrated

Across these projects, I have repeatedly worked through a cycle of:

Understand the learner→Identify the learning challenge→Structure the content→Select the learning method→Create the experience→Assess application→Review outcome→Improve design

This is the common thread connecting my work across engineering education, computing, creativity and professional learning.

24. Portfolio Closing Statement

I design learning, not just lessons.

My experience spans situations where the curriculum already existed and needed a better learning experience, as well as situations where I designed the course itself. Across engineering, computing and creativity, I have developed learning journeys that combine content, visualisation, practice, problem solving, projects, assessment and technology.

The subject may change—from mechanisms to programming to creativity—but my design question remains consistent:

What does the learner need to be able to do, and what is the most effective experience through which I can help them get there?
