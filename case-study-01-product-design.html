In practice, this meant understanding the learners and capability requirements, structuring the learning journey and outcomes, developing activities and resources, facilitating the experience through projects and hands-on work, and evaluating learning through observable performance, artifacts, testing and outcomes. The case therefore demonstrates instructional design as a complete learning system, not simply delivery of technical content.

01

The Challenge

Learner Analysis Capability Gap Analysis Outcome-Oriented Design

The challenge was not simply to teach another engineering subject. It was to help learners integrate knowledge from multiple disciplines and use it to solve unfamiliar, real-world problems.

The core learning challenge
How do we help learners integrate existing knowledge and use it to solve an unfamiliar, real-world problem?

Starting point

The primary learners were Mechanical Engineering students in Semesters 5 and 6, approximately 30 learners per batch. They already had foundations in manufacturing processes, CAD, material science, metrology, thermal engineering, thermodynamics, fluid mechanics, solid mechanics, machine drawing and drafting, machine design, and theory and kinematics of machines.

The capability gap

Learners possessed considerable subject knowledge, but advanced product-design thinking and integrated product development had not yet been sufficiently developed. The design problem therefore became one of integration: moving from separate bodies of knowledge toward a coherent process for identifying a need, defining a problem, creating a solution and demonstrating that the solution could work.

From knowledge to real-world outcome
Knowledge→ Creative Thinking→ Problem Solving→ Design→ Prototype→ Testing→ Real-World Outcome
The learning experience was designed around an integrated problem-to-product journey.

Why a different learning experience was needed

Rather than treating Product Design as another theory-based subject, the experience was designed so that learners would move from identifying a real need to developing, testing and communicating a potential solution. The intended measure of learning was not simply whether a learner could explain a concept, but whether the learner could create something meaningful from it.

02

Learning Journey

Design Thinking Experiential Learning Problem-Based Learning Project-Based Learning Interdisciplinary Learning

I designed the experience as a two-semester journey rather than treating product development as a single project at the end of a course.

Semester 1 — Discover, Define & Design

Learners explored users and problems, defined the opportunity, generated concepts, developed designs and moved toward an initial physical representation.

Semester 2 — Develop, Test & Commercialise

Learners progressed from early concepts toward more sophisticated prototypes, testing, refinement and consideration of the product's business potential.

Overall problem-to-product journey
Real-World Problem→ Empathize→ Define→ Ideate→ Product Design→ CAD→ Prototyping→ Testing→ Refinement→ Business Setup→ Product / IP / Research / Industry / Commercialisation

Learning-by-doing architecture

The instructional approach was intentionally experiential, problem-based and project-based. Learners were required to investigate problems, understand users, formulate problem statements, generate ideas, communicate ideas visually, build representations, test assumptions, refine solutions, present and defend their thinking, and consider product and business potential.

Assessment as evidence of capability

A significant design decision was to move away from relying primarily on conventional 2–3 hour written examinations. Project-based and problem-based assessment allowed learners to demonstrate capability through actual work.

03

Representative Innovation

Challenge-Based Learning Experiential Learning Coaching & Guided Practice Authentic Application

The strongest illustration of the learning-to-innovation model is the interdisciplinary Spine Mobilization Device project, which progressed from a learner-originated concept to product development, testing, IP, funding, manufacturing and commercialisation.

Flagship example — Spine Mobilization Device

The project emerged from collaboration between Mechanical Engineering and Physiotherapy. The problem explored was whether an ergonomically designed device could assist spinal mobilization while reducing dependence on repetitive thumb and hand use by physiotherapists.

Learner-originated solution

The learner team developed the initial concept. My role was to help transform the concept into a technically feasible and manufacturable product.

Interdisciplinary development

Mechanical Engineering contributed design, CAD, materials, prototyping and manufacturing expertise; Physiotherapy contributed user need, clinical context, application and testing.
